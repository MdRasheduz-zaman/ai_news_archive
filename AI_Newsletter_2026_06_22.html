Hi, There!

This week's theme is Science's New Power Centers. The biggest story isn't a new model or a new trial — it's a person. John Jumper, the Nobel laureate who led AlphaFold, announced he's leaving Google DeepMind for Anthropic after nearly nine years, and the move says more about where AI-driven biology is heading than any single paper could. Meanwhile, the infrastructure underneath that shift kept compounding: AlphaGenome quietly crossed a million API calls a day, the BIO International Convention opened in San Diego with 22+ sessions on where AI actually pays off in biotech, and a Doudna-founded CRISPR company picked up public funding for an AI-assisted gene-editing platform. The thread connecting it all: scientific firepower — talent, data, and capital — is consolidating into a smaller number of very well-resourced places.

🚀 BIG STORY OF THE WEEK

Nobel Laureate John Jumper Leaves DeepMind for Anthropic, Capping a Year of Quiet AI-for-Science Infrastructure Building

On June 19, 2026, John Jumper announced on X that he is leaving Google DeepMind after nearly nine years to join Anthropic. Jumper said he plans to take time to recharge before starting in his new role; neither he nor Anthropic has disclosed what that role will be.

Jumper led the AlphaFold team at DeepMind and shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis and the University of Washington's David Baker, for work on protein structure prediction and design. AlphaFold is one of the clearest examples of AI producing direct, measurable scientific value: its public database now offers more than 200 million protein structure predictions, used by millions of researchers worldwide as basic infrastructure for biology — the equivalent of a periodic table for protein shape.

Why this matters: This isn't a random departure. Throughout 2026, Anthropic has been methodically building what amounts to AI-for-science infrastructure: opening wet labs, publishing research on AI agents for biological workflows, and announcing flagship partnerships in February with the Allen Institute and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute (which runs the Janelia Research Campus). Those partnerships embed Claude-powered agents directly into scientific data pipelines — targeting the months-long bottlenecks in single-cell genomics, connectomics, and imaging analysis that currently separate raw experimental data from validated biological insight. Anthropic is hosting a virtual event, "The Briefing: AI for Science," on June 30 — Jumper's hire reads as a marker of intent ahead of it. Recruiting the individual scientist most associated with AI's biggest biology win to date is as much a statement about where the next one will come from as it is a hire.

Key takeaways:

  • Jumper departs DeepMind after nearly nine years, joining Anthropic in an undisclosed role
  • He shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold, now underpinning 200M+ protein structure predictions used worldwide
  • The move follows a year of Anthropic infrastructure-building: wet labs, biology agent research, and February partnerships with the Allen Institute and HHMI/Janelia
  • Anthropic streams "The Briefing: AI for Science" on June 30 — likely the next signal of how serious this bet is

📚 Top Research Papers

Computational Methods and Challenges in Cell-Free DNA Analysis for Multi-Cancer Early Detection

arXiv 2606.20174 | June 18, 2026

A comprehensive review of computational methods (2022–2025) for detecting cancer from cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in a single blood draw — covering how fragmentomics and epigenetic features are extracted and analyzed across classical statistical methods, machine learning, and deep learning frameworks including autoencoder-based models. Evaluates each approach on biological interpretability, validation rigor, and clinical-integration readiness.

Impact: Multi-cancer early detection (MCED) from a single blood draw is one of the highest-leverage applications of AI in oncology, since it targets cancers with no existing screening program. This review is a useful map for any team building or evaluating an MCED pipeline, and its call for standardized evaluation protocols highlights a real gap: without comparable benchmarks, clinical adoption decisions are currently being made on apples-to-oranges evidence.

Liquid Biopsy

GEN-Guard: Correcting Generalization Failures for Deployable Federated Surgical AI

arXiv 2606.20303 | June 18, 2026

Identifies and fixes a specific failure mode in federated learning for surgical video AI: "performance leakage," where the model selected as best during training overfits to the hospitals in the federation and fails on unseen institutions. GEN-Guard adds client-blocked evaluation to catch the leakage and disagreement-aware distillation to correct it — showing model-selection failures exceeding 80% under standard evaluation, substantially corrected on laparoscopic cholecystectomy and colonoscopy polyp segmentation benchmarks.

Impact: Federated learning is the standard answer to "how do we train hospital AI without sharing patient data" — but this paper shows the standard way of picking the "winning" federated model is quietly broken more often than not. An 80%+ model-selection failure rate is a serious, previously under-recognized deployment risk hiding inside a technique many health systems already treat as solved.

Surgical AI

HECKTOR 2025: Benchmark of Segmentation, Diagnosis, and Prognosis in Multimodal PET/CT

arXiv 2606.20143 | June 18, 2026

Results from the HECKTOR 2025 challenge, a multi-institutional benchmark (1,100+ patients, 10 centers worldwide) for automated head and neck cancer analysis from PET/CT imaging and electronic health records. Thirty-five teams competed across tumor/lymph-node segmentation, recurrence-free survival prediction, and HPV status classification. Top results: 0.75 Dice score for segmentation, 0.66 concordance index for survival, 0.56 balanced accuracy for HPV classification.

Impact: Head and neck cancer treatment planning depends on labor-intensive manual tumor delineation with high inter-observer variability — exactly the kind of task automated segmentation should fix. The relatively modest HPV-classification accuracy is the more interesting result: an honest signal that noninvasive HPV status prediction from imaging alone isn't there yet, which matters because HPV status changes treatment intensity decisions in the clinic today.

Oncology Imaging

MedRLM: Recursive Multimodal Health Intelligence for Long-Context Clinical Reasoning

arXiv 2606.20164 | June 18, 2026

Proposes a framework for clinical decision support that treats a patient's case as an environment to be recursively inspected rather than a single prompt to be answered. MedRLM coordinates specialized agents across clinical text, longitudinal EHR data, imaging, physiological sensor streams, and guideline retrieval, connected through a shared "Clinical Evidence Graph" memory — with deeper reasoning triggered automatically when abnormal patterns are detected, and uncertainty-gated handoff to clinicians for high-risk cases.

Impact: Most clinical LLM systems still compress a patient's entire record into one prompt, which breaks down for complex, longitudinal cases — exactly where decision support is needed most. The agent-decomposition approach here is architecturally similar to where general-purpose AI agent design has been heading, applied to the specific constraint set (auditability, uncertainty-gating, referral logic) that clinical deployment actually requires.

⚠️ AI Criticism & Concerns

The Jumper Hire Is a Symptom of a Bigger Talent Concentration Problem

Recruiting the AlphaFold lead is a genuine coup for Anthropic, but it's also a data point in a trend researchers have been flagging for years: AI talent is clustering inside a shrinking number of frontier labs, and the salary gap between industry and academia (now in the seven figures at the top end) makes that pull structural, not incidental. Global mobility of highly skilled AI talent fell nearly 12% in 2025 even as competition for that talent intensified. Universities historically diffused knowledge through open publication and PhD training; concentrated inside a handful of commercial labs, the same expertise increasingly stays proprietary. AlphaFold itself was a triumph partly because DeepMind published and open-sourced it. Whether the next AlphaFold-scale breakthrough gets the same treatment is now a genuinely open question.

The FDA's Own Officials Don't Trust "Approve Once, Trust Forever"

Internal FDA pushback against proposals to ease AI medical device oversight made a sharp point this year: approving one AI device from a company doesn't demonstrate that the company's future products will be safe, and leaning too heavily on manufacturers' internal monitoring systems leaves the agency with insufficient independent visibility. That's a reasonable, conservative position — but it sits in tension with an industry moving toward continuous model updates, where the traditional one-time approval model is already a poor fit. Neither side of this has been fully resolved, and hospitals are deploying AI tools in that gap right now.

Healthcare AI Regulation Is Becoming 50 Different Compliance Problems

With no comprehensive federal AI statute, states have taken the lead: 21 states had enacted 33 healthcare-AI-specific laws by the end of 2025, and lawmakers across 47 states introduced more than 250 related bills that year. For any health system or AI vendor operating across state lines — which is most of them — that's not a regulatory environment, it's a compliance maze that changes underneath them every legislative session. Patchwork regulation protects patients unevenly by geography, which is its own kind of inequity.

Clinicians Are Carrying Liability Risk That Policy Hasn't Caught Up To Yet

As AI recommender systems increasingly shape clinical decisions, the liability question — who's responsible when a clinician follows a flawed AI recommendation — remains genuinely unsettled. A UK National Commission's formal recommendations on this aren't expected until summer 2026, which means the policy answer is arriving well after the tools are already embedded in clinical workflows. Until liability frameworks catch up, individual clinicians are effectively absorbing systemic risk that AI vendors and health system administrators created.

Closing Note

Most weeks in this newsletter, the headline is a result: a trial readout, a funding round, a benchmark score. This week's headline is a person changing employers — and that's precisely what makes it worth sitting with. AlphaFold wasn't built by one person, but John Jumper became its public face because the work was genuinely his to lead, and where he goes next is a reasonable proxy for where the field's center of gravity is moving.

What's underneath that move is the more durable story: Anthropic spent a year quietly assembling wet labs, biology agents, and HHMI/Allen Institute partnerships before this hire ever became public. AlphaGenome's adoption curve, BIO 2026's AI-ROI benchmarking, and Scribe Therapeutics' publicly funded AI-CRISPR platform are all pointing at the same underlying shift: the organizations that can afford to combine top scientific talent, massive compute, and proprietary data are pulling away from everyone else, including the universities that trained that talent in the first place.

None of this makes the science less real — AlphaFold's 200 million structures and AlphaGenome's million daily queries are genuine public goods, freely available right now. But "freely available" and "openly developed" are different properties, and as the next generation of tools gets built inside private labs with Nobel laureates on staff, it's worth watching closely which one survives.
