Issue #38  |  Week of July 7, 2026

Hi, There!

Two weeks' worth of news to catch up on, and the throughline is unmistakable: the AI companies aren't content supplying tools to biology and medicine anymore — they're becoming biology and medicine companies themselves. On June 30, Anthropic launched Claude Science and announced it will develop its own drugs for diseases Big Pharma won't touch. Days later, Stanford's Biomni showed an AI agent operating as a genuine biomedical co-scientist in a peer-reviewed Science paper. OpenAI dropped a genomics benchmark hard enough that its own best model only solves it 3 times in 10. And in the clinic, an AI-designed vaccine cleared its first human safety trial while an AI liver model became the first computational tool the FDA has ever formally recognized as a drug development instrument. This issue covers the best of both weeks — June 30 through July 13.

🚀 BIG STORY OF THE WEEK

Anthropic Launches Claude Science and Its Own Drug Discovery Program

On June 30, Anthropic unveiled Claude Science, a research workbench built specifically for scientists and pharmaceutical R&D teams — and, in the same announcement, revealed it is standing up an internal drug discovery program of its own.

What Claude Science does:

  • Integrates 60+ scientific databases and connectors across genomics, proteomics, structural biology, and cheminformatics into one environment
  • Connects established tools researchers already use — PubMed, Jupyter, R — instead of replacing them
  • Runs autonomously on high-level instructions, tracing every artifact back to its underlying code and calculations via built-in reviewer agents that check citations and computations
  • Manages compute environments on demand, so a researcher can move from literature review to a running analysis without switching tools

The drug discovery angle is the more striking part. Anthropic's life sciences head, Eric Kauderer-Abrams, said the company will target "neglected" diseases that traditional biopharma considers commercially unattractive. His rationale: "We're doing this because we believe first and foremost that to build the right models, products and tools to accelerate the industry, we need to live it along with all of you." In other words — Anthropic is eating its own dog food by becoming a drug developer, not just a drug-development tool vendor.

Anthropic is also funding outside researchers directly: up to $30,000 in Claude Science credits for up to 50 external research projects, with applications open through July 15, 2026.

Why this matters: This is the second major AI-lab-turned-life-sciences-infrastructure move in three weeks, following NVIDIA's BioNeMo Agent Toolkit launch at BIO2026. Two of the industry's most consequential AI companies are now racing to become indispensable — and increasingly primary — players inside biology and drug development, not just suppliers to it.

Key takeaways:

  • Claude Science launched June 30, 2026 as a beta research workbench for Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise users
  • Anthropic's internal drug discovery arm will target neglected diseases first
  • $30,000 in research credits available to external scientists through July 15
  • Follows a broader pattern: frontier AI labs are no longer just tool providers to biotech — they're becoming biotech companies

📚 Top Research Papers

Autonomous Biomedical Research with an Artificial Intelligence Agent (Biomni)

Huang, Zou, Leskovec et al. | Science | July 2026

Introduces Biomni, a general-purpose biomedical AI agent that autonomously plans and executes multi-step research workflows — retrieving from 59 databases, running 105 software packages, and selecting from 150 specialized tools spanning 25 biomedical subdomains. Demonstrated strong generalization across causal gene prioritization, drug repurposing, rare-disease diagnosis, microbiome analysis, and molecular cloning without any task-specific fine-tuning.

Impact: The first peer-reviewed, general-purpose (not narrowly-scoped) biomedical research agent — a direct proof point that autonomous AI can meaningfully accelerate the "tedious legwork" bottleneck in biomedical discovery, not just automate a single narrow task.

Autonomous Research Agent

ALICE: Learning a General-Purpose Pathology Foundation Model from Vision, Vision-Language, and Slide-Level Experts

Li, Guan, Shi et al. | arXiv 2607.09526 | July 10, 2026

ALICE unifies eight separate specialized pathology teacher models — vision-only, vision-language, and slide-level — into a single foundation model via multi-stage agglomerative distillation. Pretrained on nearly 25 million tile-level pathology images, it was evaluated across 96 downstream tasks and 48 data sources, achieving the best average rank among task-matched pathology foundation models in every evaluation setting.

Impact: Consolidating fragmented, specialist pathology models into one backbone is exactly the kind of infrastructure work needed to make AI-assisted cancer diagnosis and biomarker discovery (like last issue's SPARK framework) practically deployable at scale, rather than requiring a different model per task.

Pathology AI

A Censoring-Aware Target Interface for Tabular Foundation Models in Survival Prediction (SurvFM-RMST)

Lyu, Lin, Huang, Li | arXiv 2607.09577 | July 10, 2026

Tabular foundation models are increasingly used for patient prognosis, but nearly all assume fully observed outcomes — a poor fit for real clinical data, where patient follow-up is frequently cut short (right-censored). SurvFM-RMST converts survival outcomes into pseudo-observation targets, letting general-purpose tabular models perform horizon-specific survival prediction without survival-specific retraining. Tested across 36 real clinical datasets.

Impact: A practical bridge letting hospitals reuse general-purpose AI models for prognosis instead of building bespoke survival models from scratch for every new dataset — directly lowering the engineering cost of deploying prognostic AI in real clinical settings.

Clinical Prognosis

Global Testing of SNP-Methylation Interactions on Binary Phenotypes via a Logistic Functional Regression Model

Gansou, Oualkacha, Cremona, Lakhal-Chaieb | arXiv 2607.09535 | July 10, 2026

Proposes a statistical framework for testing whether interactions between DNA methylation and genetic variants (SNPs) jointly influence disease risk, modeling methylation as a smooth functional predictor rather than isolated CpG sites. Shows well-calibrated error rates and improved statistical power over classical pairwise SNP-methylation tests, demonstrated on an obesity case-control dataset.

Impact: Most genetic-epigenetic interaction studies test one SNP-CpG pair at a time, missing weaker but real region-level effects. This region-level approach could surface disease mechanisms that pairwise testing — the current standard in most clinical genomics pipelines — simply cannot detect.

⚠️ AI Criticism & Concerns

Anthropic is now grading its own homework

Anthropic builds the AI model, builds the Claude Science tooling, and is now using both to develop its own drugs. When the same company controls the model, the platform, and the pharma pipeline built on top of it, external validation gets structurally harder — who audits a drug candidate discovered by a company evaluating its own AI's output? The "neglected diseases" framing is genuinely good PR and possibly good medicine, but it doesn't resolve the conflict-of-interest question at the core of vertically integrating AI-lab-plus-drugmaker.

GeneBench-Pro's low scores are being under-covered relative to the launch hype

A 31.5% pass rate on real genomics problems — meaning the best available model still fails roughly 7 out of 10 questions a human expert solves in a day's work — is a sobering number that got far less attention than the flashy "AI co-scientist" and "PhD-level agent" framing dominating this same two-week period (Biomni, Claude Science, BioNeMo). The gap between marketing language and benchmarked reality in computational biology AI remains wide, and worth remembering before any of these tools get near a real clinical or drug-development decision.

FDA's Digital Liver Model precedent moves fast, with limited public transparency

Absentia Labs' Digital Liver Model becoming the first AI drug-development tool in the FDA's ISTAND program is a landmark — but the underlying model's training data, validation methodology, and failure modes are not fully public. If this qualification becomes a template other AI toxicology tools follow, the industry needs a public benchmark for what "qualified" AI safety tooling actually requires, not just a single company's internal claims plus a three-stage bureaucratic process most outside observers can't inspect.

AI mental health apps are proliferating faster than any clinical validation can follow

This two-week window alone produced at least four new AI-powered mental health and mood-tracking products (Andy, Emote, ZenGen, Chum Wellness) launching on Product Hunt with zero peer-reviewed efficacy data. Some explicitly position themselves as tools to "bring into your next therapy session" — a reasonable, complementary use case — but the line between "structured self-reflection tool" and "unlicensed therapeutic intervention" is blurring faster than regulators or clinical researchers can study it.

Closing Note

Two weeks compressed into one issue, but the pattern holds regardless of the window: the biggest AI labs no longer want to be vendors to biology and medicine — they want to be biology and medicine companies. Anthropic is developing its own drugs. NVIDIA (last issue) gave agents a pharma research lab's toolkit. Stanford proved in Science that a general-purpose agent can already do real biomedical research work. And OpenAI, to its credit, published a benchmark honest enough to show its own best model still fails most of what a working genomics scientist handles in an afternoon.

That last point matters most. The Cambridge vaccine and the FDA's liver model both represent AI succeeding at a narrow, well-defined task with real clinical stakes and real validation behind them. GeneBench-Pro is a reminder that "narrow and validated" and "general and autonomous" are very different bars — and most of what's launching right now, however impressively branded, is still closer to the first than the second.
