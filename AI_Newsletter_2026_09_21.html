Issue #47  |  Week of September 15, 2026

Hi, There!

Two weeks ago ARPA-H asked who's accountable as AI gets closer to the moment of clinical decision-making, and built a supervisory AI just to watch the answer. This week we got a much less theoretical version of the same question: FOIA documents revealed that Medicare's WISeR AI prior-authorization pilot shipped on schedule even after a vendor warned CMS a working product wasn't realistic in time — and patients and doctors across six states have been living with the consequences since January. Meanwhile Anthropic confirmed it runs a physical biology lab and struck a drug-discovery partnership with Novo Nordisk, a multi-country trial found GPT-4o closes diagnostic-accuracy gaps most for physicians in lower-income countries, and the FDA cleared its first foundation-model-based chest X-ray AI. Same theme as ever: the technology keeps compounding faster than the accountability structures around it.

🚀 BIG STORY OF THE WEEK

FOIA Documents Reveal Medicare's WISeR AI Prior-Authorization Pilot Was Rushed, Leaving Patients and Doctors in Limbo

On September 15, 2026, STAT News reported on over 1,000 pages of documents obtained by the Electronic Frontier Foundation through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit, exposing the rushed rollout of CMS's WISeR (Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction) AI prior-authorization pilot. According to the documents, a WISeR vendor warned CMS before launch that meeting the agency's timeline with a functional product was unrealistic — and CMS launched anyway.

What WISeR does: The pilot went live in January 2026 across six states — New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona, and Washington — using AI to screen Medicare claims for procedures like skin substitutes and epidural injections before approving them. It runs through 2031, and CMS has signaled it could expand nationally.

The human cost: KFF Health News and CBS News have spent months documenting the fallout. A 65-year-old Oklahoma cattle farmer has been forced into extra, unplanned trips for quarterly epidural treatments. Nearly 100 patients were queued waiting on epidural-injection authorizations at a single University of Washington clinic. A New Jersey patient went to the hospital for costlier emergency care rather than wait on an approval. "It's been horrendous. Right from the beginning, there seemed to be no organization," a Phoenix pain-management doctor told KFF Health News.

The errors compound the delays: An Arizona physician received a denial citing "patient ineligibility" for an entirely different body region than the one requested. An Oklahoma radiologist documented a denial that cited a symptom — numbness — his patient didn't have, on four separate occasions.

The incentive problem underneath: The Center for Medicare Advocacy has separately flagged that WISeR's vendors are compensated based on the "savings" they generate — an incentive structure critics say mirrors the prior-authorization abuses that push many seniors toward traditional Medicare specifically to avoid this exact experience.

Why this matters: ADVOCATE, two weeks ago, was AI accountability as a forward-looking engineering problem — how do you build a system to police an autonomous AI before something goes wrong. WISeR is what happens when that discipline is skipped: an AI decision system deployed under a hard deadline, with no working product to show for it, running unsupervised on real patients' care for eight months before anyone outside CMS could see the paper trail.

Key takeaways:

  • FOIA documents show a WISeR vendor warned CMS pre-launch that a working product wasn't realistic on the agency's timeline
  • WISeR has run since January 2026 across 6 states and is authorized through 2031, with possible national expansion
  • Real patients have faced delayed care, extra unplanned visits, and documented AI review errors
  • Vendors are paid based on "savings" generated — an incentive structure Medicare advocates call structurally misaligned with patient interests

⚡ Quick Updates

  • Anthropic Confirms It's Running a Bay Area Wet Lab for Physical Biology Experiments: On September 18, TechCrunch confirmed Anthropic operates a physical biology lab in the Bay Area where its AI models help design and interpret real experiments, alongside a Life Sciences Verification Program giving vetted outside researchers access to its most capable models. "The final test is still, and will be for a while, in real lab work," said Anthropic's head of life sciences. The company says the lab focuses on fundamental biology rather than drug discovery specifically, and it follows Anthropic's $400M acquisition of Coefficient Bio in April. TechCrunch
  • Novo Nordisk and Anthropic Launch “Claude Science” Drug-Discovery Partnership: Announced September 16, Novo Nordisk and Anthropic are partnering to embed Claude across Novo's drug discovery and development pipeline, aiming to speed up timelines from target identification through trial design. Novo Nordisk CEO Mike Doustdar and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei both emphasized "robust data governance and human oversight" in announcing the deal, though neither company disclosed its financial terms. Novo Nordisk
  • Multi-Country Trial: GPT-4o Lifts Physician Accuracy Most in Lower-Income Countries: A randomized controlled trial published September 9 in npj Digital Medicine tested 249 physicians in Indonesia, Kenya, and the Netherlands, comparing diagnostic performance with and without GPT-4o access. Access improved scores by 18 percentage points in Kenya, 10.7 in Indonesia, and 7.2 in the Netherlands (all p<0.001) — the largest gains in the lower-resourced settings. Authors caution the results come from controlled testing rather than live clinics, but say the pattern suggests "LLMs may help reduce disparities in care quality" globally. npj Digital Medicine
  • FDA Clears DeepHealth's Foundation-Model Chest X-Ray AI: On September 18, the FDA cleared DeepHealth's Chest XRay software, among the first FDA-cleared chest X-ray AI platforms built on a foundation model rather than a narrowly-trained classifier. It detects and localizes thoracic abnormalities — lung nodules, pneumothorax, mediastinal abnormalities, and consolidation — as an adjunctive read for radiologists. "Foundation models are important building blocks… [this] demonstrates how a broadly trained foundation model can be translated into a precise, FDA-cleared clinical capability today," said DeepHealth COO/CTO Sham Sokka. 📚 Top Research Papers

GIA: Germline-Informed Aging with AlphaGenome Finds Genetically Regulated CpGs

Sean Lim | arXiv 2609.17801 | September 17, 2026

Uses DeepMind's AlphaGenome model to show that the CpG methylation sites underlying widely used "epigenetic clocks" for biological-age estimation are substantially shaped by inherited genetic variation, not just aging — clock CpGs were enriched for blood methylation-QTLs relative to unused control probes (62.7% vs. 39.6%), with specific variants (including one near FHL2) shown to shift a clock's baseline reading independent of actual aging.

Impact: A direct challenge to how epigenetic clocks are calibrated and interpreted — and a timely one, arriving weeks after DeepMind's own AlphaGenome Atlas release put genome-wide variant predictions into researchers' hands at scale.

Epigenetic Aging

An Immune World Model for Multiscale Forecasting and Therapeutic Hypothesis Generation

Taoyong Cui, Xi Wang, Zonghang Li, Jinchao Ding, Lingsen You, Yuzhi Xu, Wanghan Xu, Fang Wu, Kejun Ying, Wanli Ouyang, Pheng Ann Heng, Ling Yang, Zhenfei Yin, Yingcheng Wu | arXiv 2609.14709 | September 14, 2026

Builds an action-conditioned "world model" — produced by an automated AI Scientist system — that learns how medical interventions ripple through the immune system across cellular, tissue, and whole-patient scales, generalizing to interventions never seen during training.

Impact: The model didn't just forecast outcomes, it generated a novel therapeutic hypothesis on its own — flagging combined IL-36γ and SIRPα inhibition as promising — a concrete example of AI proposing, not just evaluating, drug combinations.

AI Scientist / Immunology

GPCR Ligand Bioactivity Prediction with Physics-Informed Dual-State Query Learning

Shuo Zhang, Huifeng Zhang, Rongqi Hong, Jian K. Liu | arXiv 2609.16468 | September 16, 2026

Introduces Dual-State Query (DSQ), a neural architecture that builds the physics of receptor activation — the Monod-Wyman-Changeux allostery model — directly into bioactivity prediction for G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), the target of roughly a third of approved drugs, via a "neural MWC gating module" that represents active vs. inactive receptor states from sequence alone.

Impact: Outperforms existing methods on agonist prediction without requiring a 3D structure, though it still struggles on receptors with little similarity to its training data — a reminder that physics-informed priors help most exactly where training data is thinnest.

⚠️ AI Criticism & Concerns

WISeR is what happens when accountability discipline gets skipped

The vendor's own pre-launch warning to CMS — and CMS launching anyway — turns "who's accountable when AI-assisted care goes wrong" from a design question into an after-the-fact one. The Center for Medicare Advocacy's finding that WISeR's vendors are paid based on "savings" generated adds a structural incentive problem on top of the technical one, and with CMS signaling possible national expansion, critics argue this pilot is exactly the cautionary tale that should shape how the next one is built — not just how this one gets fixed.

Anthropic's wet lab reopens the “safety first” contradiction debate

News that Anthropic runs a physical biology lab drew pointed skepticism over the optics of a company that has repeatedly warned about catastrophic AI risk — including a researcher, Jacob Coxon, whose resignation letter warned AI "could kill us all by the end of the decade" — simultaneously running hands-on biology experimentation. Investor Chamath Palihapitiya mocked the juxtaposition publicly. Anthropic says the lab is about fundamental biology, not drug discovery, but the episode underscores an unresolved tension for safety-focused labs expanding into higher-stakes physical-world domains.

Every new FDA AI clearance sits on top of an unresolved liability gap

This week's DeepHealth chest X-ray clearance is one of well over 1,300 FDA-cleared AI medical devices — but fewer than 2% carry independent clinical-trial evidence, since most clear through the 510(k) "substantial equivalence" pathway, and no federal liability framework yet defines who's responsible when one of these systems contributes to patient harm. Independent benchmarks have found real-world accuracy can fall sharply outside standardized testing conditions. Geoffrey Hinton has pointed to the perverse incentive this creates: a doctor faces liability whether or not they use AI, but using it may expose them to liability that feels more "immediate" if harm follows — a gap regulators, insurers, and hospitals are still racing to close even as clearances keep coming.

Closing Note

Two weeks ago, ARPA-H's answer to "who's accountable" was to build a second AI whose entire job is supervising the first one. This week, WISeR showed what happens without that discipline: a vendor's own warning ignored, a hard launch date kept anyway, and eight months of real patients absorbing the gap between "we shipped on time" and "it actually works." Anthropic's wet lab and its Novo Nordisk partnership are a reminder that the industry is racing toward deeper biological integration at the same time regulatory and liability frameworks are still catching up to the AI tools already on the market — DeepHealth's chest X-ray clearance is just the latest of over 1,300 with that gap still unresolved underneath it.

The GPT-4o equity finding is the one genuinely hopeful data point this week: AI access closing diagnostic gaps most for physicians with the fewest existing resources is exactly the outcome worth building toward. The question, as always, is whether the accountability structures catch up before the next WISeR does.
