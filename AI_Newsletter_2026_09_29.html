Hi, There!

Two weeks ago we told you Anthropic runs a physical biology lab. Last week we watched Medicare's AI prior-authorization pilot show what happens without accountability discipline. This week the biology lab produced its first headline result: Claude spent 21 hours and 950 agents searching a DNA database and surfaced a previously uncharacterized, CRISPR-like enzyme system in bacteriophages — cheered by some scientists as a preview of AI-accelerated discovery, and waved off by others as a pattern match awaiting actual peer review. Meanwhile Anthropic and OpenEvidence opened free physician AI search to roughly 100 lower-income countries, two more AI-biotech rounds closed (Enveda at $311M, Basecamp Research at $140M) on top of a sector that still has zero FDA-approved AI-discovered drugs to show for its billions, and the FDA cleared an AI reading tool for capsule endoscopy. Same throughline as always: extraordinary claims arriving faster than anyone can verify them.

🚀 BIG STORY OF THE WEEK

Anthropic Says Claude Autonomously Discovered a Novel CRISPR-Like Enzyme System in Bacteriophages

On September 23, 2026, Anthropic announced that Claude had identified a previously uncharacterized enzyme system in bacteriophage DNA — named ART (array-associated reverse transcriptases) — through a hybrid AI-plus-wet-lab process the company is holding up as a preview of AI-accelerated science.

How the discovery happened: In a computational phase, roughly 950 Claude agents spent 21 hours and 210 million tokens searching a massive DNA sequence database for interesting reverse-transcriptase families. One agent flagged a DNA region sitting next to a reverse transcriptase gene, noting: "[The DNA next to the RT] is spectacular: I can see by eye a tandem repeat array" — the same kind of repeating structure that defines CRISPR systems. Human scientists then moved into Anthropic's Bay Area wet lab (BSL-1/BSL-2) — the same lab confirmed two weeks ago — to biochemically and structurally validate the finding.

What ART actually is: Three components — a reverse transcriptase enzyme, a partner gene, and a long array of evenly spaced DNA repeats. Initial experiments show that repeat array is expressed as distinct short RNAs, hinting at programmable, CRISPR-like functionality, similar in spirit to how CRISPR's guide RNAs work. Anthropic has not yet determined what ART actually does biologically.

Why publish now instead of waiting: Anthropic released a technical preprint rather than a peer-reviewed paper, explicitly to invite outside scrutiny and collaboration rather than sit on the finding.

The reaction is split: Stanford bioengineering professor Stanley Qi called it "incredibly exciting," saying AI could "expand our ability to explore" complex biological patterns "more effectively and rapidly." Washington University microbiologist Kevin Blake was far more skeptical: "There's nothing to indicate this is a rival to CRISPR-the-technology, or could be developed into any kind of therapeutic application" — noting CRISPR-like repeat sequences have long been observable across millions of unstudied bacterial species, so finding one is not, by itself, the hard part.

Why this matters: This is the direct payoff of the wet-lab story we flagged two weeks ago, and it's a genuinely useful test case for the accountability question this newsletter keeps returning to: an AI lab announcing its own AI's scientific discovery, via its own preprint, before independent replication — a very different accountability structure than a regulator-reviewed clinical pilot like WISeR, and worth watching for whether outside labs confirm ART's function or quietly let it fade.

Key takeaways:

  • ~950 Claude agents searched a DNA database for 21 hours (210M tokens) and flagged a novel CRISPR-like repeat structure next to a reverse transcriptase gene
  • Anthropic's Bay Area wet lab validated the finding biochemically; the system's actual biological function is still unknown
  • Published as a preprint, not peer-reviewed, specifically to invite outside scrutiny
  • Expert reaction is split between "genuinely exciting exploration tool" and "pattern match, not a breakthrough"

⚡ Quick Updates

  • Anthropic and OpenEvidence Launch Free Physician AI Search for ~100 Low- and Middle-Income Countries: Announced September 22-23, Anthropic and OpenEvidence are jointly offering a specialized, no-cost version of OpenEvidence's medical-literature search engine to healthcare providers across roughly 100 countries — including Angola, Haiti, Mongolia, Sudan, and Uganda — that otherwise lack ready access to peer-reviewed research and ongoing education. "Access to medical knowledge shouldn't depend on geography," said OpenEvidence founder Daniel Nadler. It follows last week's GPT-4o trial showing the biggest diagnostic-accuracy gains for physicians in lower-resourced settings. PYMNTS
  • Enveda Raises $311M Series E to Push AI-Discovered Natural Medicines Into Late-Stage Trials: On September 23, Enveda Biosciences — which uses AI to mine plants and microbes for drug candidates rather than synthesizing them from scratch — closed a $311M Series E at a $2B valuation, double its mark a year earlier. It now has multiple candidates in human trials, including one for severe dermatological conditions and another for preserving weight loss after patients stop GLP-1 medications. No AI-discovered medicine has yet received FDA approval. TechCrunch
  • Basecamp Research Raises $140M, Backed by Anthropic and NVIDIA, for AI-Designed Cell Therapies: On September 23-24, Basecamp Research closed a $140M Series C led by S32, with NVIDIA, Anthropic, and Roche Vice Chairman André Hoffmann participating. Its EDEN model, trained on the "Trillion Gene Atlas" biological dataset, generates candidate peptides, enzymes, and cell and gene therapies, with an initial focus on in vivo cell therapies that reprogram patients' cells from inside the body. "Personalized, AI-designed therapeutics represent the next transformation" of the biotech revolution, said Hoffmann. BioSpace
  • FDA Clears CapsoVision's AI Highlights for Capsule Endoscopy: On September 28, the FDA cleared CapsoVision's "AI Highlights," an AI-assisted reading tool for its CapsoCam Plus capsule endoscopy system that automatically flags suspected abnormal lesions in small-bowel images, for adult and pediatric patients (age 2+) suspected of small-bowel bleeding. "FDA clearance of AI Highlights represents an important milestone in the continued evolution of the CapsoVision platform," said CEO Johnny Wang. GlobeNewswire

📚 Top Research Papers

WILSON — A Pathology Foundation Model Framework for Patient-Level Analysis and Diagnostic Text Generation

Saghir Alfasly, Wataru Uegami, Sobhan Hemati, Wenchao Han, Xiaojia Tang, Kevin Thompson, Daniel Stone, Ghazal Alabtah, Saba Yasir, Michael R. Lucas, Eric W. Klee, Cheryl L. Willman, Judy C. Boughey, Matthew P. Goetz, Krishna R. Kalari, H.R. Tizhoosh | arXiv 2609.25123 | September 2026

Represents an entire whole-slide pathology image as a compact multi-magnification composite instead of thousands of individual tiles, dramatically cutting the compute needed for slide-level analysis. Trained on roughly 189,000 Mayo Clinic slides, it exceeded a dedicated case-level baseline on all internal cohorts while requiring far fewer resources, and supports diagnostic retrieval, cross-modal image-text matching, and automated pathology report generation.

Impact: A rare foundation-model paper that gets more efficient rather than less as it scales to patient-level (not just tile-level) analysis — exactly the direction digital pathology needs to move to be clinically deployable rather than a research-only benchmark exercise.

Digital Pathology

Correlation-Guided Flow Matching with Annealed Masking for Spatial Transcriptomics Generation

Yupei Zhang, Li Pan, Hao Chen, Chao Li, Xiaohan Xing | arXiv 2609.22187 | September 22, 2026

Introduces CorrFlow, a generative framework for predicting spatial gene-expression profiles directly from histology images. Existing methods largely treat genes as independent; CorrFlow combines annealed masked flow matching with gene-graph regularization to explicitly model gene-gene dependencies, producing more biologically coherent expression profiles than prior approaches.

Impact: Spatial transcriptomics is expensive and slow to generate directly — a model that predicts it accurately from routine histology images could make spatial gene-expression data dramatically more accessible to labs that can't run the wet-lab assay themselves.

Spatial Transcriptomics

QLoRA Fine-Tuning of Ministral LLM for Sequence-to-Function Protein Annotation

Demian Pavlyshenko, Bohdan Pavlyshenko | arXiv 2609.24538 | September 21, 2026

Fine-tunes a compact 3-billion-parameter language model with QLoRA, a parameter-efficient technique, to generate curator-style functional annotations directly from raw protein sequences.

Impact: The authors find genuine biological value in a substantial subset of outputs, but flag hallucination risk as a real deployment barrier — a useful, honest counterpoint to this week's Big Story about trusting AI-generated biological claims at face value.

Protein Annotation

Uncertainty Quantification-Incorporated Ensemble Model for Personalized Prediction of Severe Radiotherapy-Induced Immunosuppression Among Esophageal Cancer Patients

Baode Gao, Yan Chu, Zongsheng Hu, Madison E. Grayson, Peter S.N. van Rossum, Clemens Grassberger, Heiko Enderling, Harald Paganetti, Ibrahim Chamseddine, Steven H. Lin, Brian P. Hobbs, Radhe Mohan, Yiqing Chen | arXiv 2609.25139 | September 2026

Builds an ML ensemble model, with formal uncertainty quantification, to predict how low a patient's lymphocyte count will fall after radiotherapy for esophageal cancer — a key marker of dangerous immune suppression.

Impact: Strong predictive accuracy paired with mathematically guaranteed prediction intervals means clinicians get a calibrated confidence range, not just a point estimate — exactly the kind of honestly-uncertain AI output that's harder to overtrust than a single confident number.

⚠️ AI Criticism & Concerns

Claude's CRISPR-Like “Discovery” Is a Self-Reported Pattern Match, Not (Yet) Peer-Reviewed Science

Anthropic released its ART enzyme-system finding as a preprint, and independent scientists are split on how much it matters. Washington University microbiologist Kevin Blake pushed back directly: "there's nothing to indicate this is a rival to CRISPR-the-technology, or could be developed into any kind of therapeutic application," noting that CRISPR-like repeat sequences have been observable across millions of unstudied bacterial genomes for years. Anthropic has not yet determined what ART actually does biologically, and the initial coverage cycle runs entirely on Anthropic's own framing before outside replication catches up.

AI Drug Discovery Has Raised Billions and Still Has Zero FDA-Approved Medicines to Show for It

This week's $311M Enveda round and $140M Basecamp Research round add to a sector that has raised roughly $8.9 billion in recent years — with not a single AI-discovered drug yet approved by the FDA, and approval-timeline projections "quietly sliding for three consecutive years." AI's genuine efficiency gains are concentrated almost entirely in preclinical stages; "the algorithm's advantage erodes against the irreducible complexity of biology" once compounds reach human trials. Investors are betting billions this decade is different; the historical base rate so far says otherwise.

OpenEvidence's Free Access for Poorer Countries Sits Next to a Quiet Pivot Into For-Profit Drug Development

The same week OpenEvidence and Anthropic publicized free clinical-AI access for roughly 100 low- and middle-income countries, OpenEvidence closed a $250M raise at a $15B valuation with far less fanfare — reportedly down from a $20B ask two months earlier — while simultaneously moving into proprietary oncology drug development, a venture that sits uneasily next to its "democratizing medical knowledge" messaging. Nothing about the free-access program is disingenuous on its own, but it raises the question of which mission ends up setting the company's priorities as it scales.

Closing Note

Three weeks running, this newsletter has watched the same accountability question show up in a different costume each time: ARPA-H built an AI to supervise an AI; WISeR showed what happens when that discipline gets skipped under a deadline; and this week, Anthropic's own lab announced its own AI's scientific discovery through its own preprint, with independent scientists immediately split on whether it's a preview of the future or a pattern match dressed up as one. None of these are the same failure mode, but they share a structure: a claim arrives faster than anyone outside the organization making it can verify.

The OpenEvidence and Enveda/Basecamp stories are the more hopeful and more sobering versions of the same pattern, respectively — free access to medical literature for doctors who've never had it is an unambiguous good, while $8.9 billion in AI drug-discovery funding with zero approvals is a reminder that "AI found it" and "AI cured it" remain very different claims. Worth tracking whether ART gets independently replicated before the next issue, and whether any of this year's AI-discovered drug candidates make it past the phase where biology usually wins.
