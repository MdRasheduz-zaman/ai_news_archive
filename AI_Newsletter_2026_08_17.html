Issue #42  |  Week of August 11, 2026

Hi, There!

On August 6, Stanford and Arc Institute researchers published something this newsletter has been circling all summer without quite reaching: an AI that didn't just analyze biology — it authored sixteen functioning viruses that have never existed in nature, and they worked. The fallout was immediate and loud: Johns Hopkins biosecurity researchers demanding legally mandated screening, and Senator Bernie Sanders sending letters to three AI CEOs threatening congressional action. Around that story, the rest of the week continued in familiar directions — Novo Nordisk signed a major AI partnership with AWS, a one-time triple therapy cleared HIV in newborn primates, and Anthropic pitched its healthcare ambitions to IPO investors while its own model remains barred from professional drug research. Capability and consequence, in the same week, at the same intensity.

🚀 BIG STORY OF THE WEEK

AI Designs Working Viruses From Scratch — And a Senator Tells Three CEOs to Stop

On August 6, 2026, Science published research from Brian Hie's lab at Stanford and the Arc Institute showing that generative genome language models — Evo 1 and Evo 2, trained on genetic sequence data spanning all domains of life — designed complete, functional bacteriophage genomes that match no known natural sequence.

What the AI actually did: The models generated roughly 700,000 candidate bacteriophage genomes. Researchers synthesized and tested about 285–300 of them. Sixteen worked — real, functioning viruses that had never existed in nature before this experiment. And they weren't just functional: they successfully killed two distinct strains of E. coli that had already evolved resistance to the naturally occurring phage.

Why this is a genuine first: This is the first time a generative AI model has designed a complete functional viral genome from scratch — not modifying an existing virus, not predicting a structure, but authoring an entire genetic blueprint that, when synthesized, produced a working biological organism.

The gap this exposed: Between an AI-designed genome and a physical vial of synthesized DNA sits exactly one checkpoint — DNA synthesis screening — and it is currently voluntary, not legally required, in the United States. Johns Hopkins biosecurity researchers responded immediately, calling for legally mandated screening of synthetic genetic-material orders rather than continued reliance on industry self-regulation.

The political response: On August 10, Senator Bernie Sanders sent letters to Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and Mark Zuckerberg, invoking the bacteriophage research directly: "In the interest of humanity, stand by your words. Pause AI development. It is not too late to avoid disaster. Stop building machines that humans cannot control." He closed with a direct threat: "If you do not take appropriate action now, my colleagues and I in the U.S. Senate will."

The context that makes this land harder: Back in June, the heads of OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, and Microsoft AI had already jointly written to Congress asking for exactly this — mandatory synthetic DNA screening legislation — warning that AI was eroding the technical barriers to weaponizing biology. Two months later, a lab demonstrated precisely the capability those CEOs had warned about, and the legislation they asked for still doesn't exist.

Why this matters: This isn't a hypothetical risk scenario anymore — it's a peer-reviewed, published result. The same generative modeling approach that could eventually design better bacteriophage therapies against antibiotic-resistant infections is, mechanically, the same approach that could someday design something far more dangerous. The therapeutic upside is real (this is genuinely promising for phage therapy). So is the fact that the only thing standing between "AI designs a genome" and "someone prints it" is a voluntary industry norm.

Key takeaways:

  • Evo 1/Evo 2 generated ~700,000 candidate genomes; ~300 were synthesized; 16 were functional, novel bacteriophages
  • The 16 phages killed antibiotic-resistant E. coli strains that had defeated the natural phage
  • DNA synthesis screening remains voluntary in the U.S., despite AI labs themselves requesting mandatory legislation in June
  • Sanders' August 10 letters to OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta explicitly threaten Senate action if the labs don't self-impose a pause

⚡ Quick Updates

  • Novo Nordisk and AWS Sign Strategic Partnership to Accelerate AI Drug Discovery: Announced August 10, the deal names AWS as Novo Nordisk's preferred cloud and strategic AI partner, with a new co-innovation hub in London aimed at compressing the path from drug target to first human dose. More than 25,000 Novo Nordisk employees are already using AWS-based AI tools, with early results including reduced clinical documentation time. Financial terms were not disclosed. GlobeNewswire
  • One-Time Triple Therapy Completely Clears HIV in Newborn Primates: Published in Nature Microbiology, a combination of standard antiretrovirals, broadly neutralizing antibodies, and the experimental antibody leronlimab completely cleared HIV in newborn nonhuman primates treated within three days of exposure — even though none of the three components worked alone. Co-lead author Jonah Sacha noted the approach "could go to clinical trials immediately" for human newborns, though substantial additional safety and dosing work remains before that's possible. ScienceDaily
  • AI Maps the Brain's Waste-Clearing System, Finds Two Distinct Speeds: Using physics-informed AI to extract fluid-flow velocities from MRI data, researchers found the brain's glymphatic system — which clears proteins like amyloid-beta, linked to Alzheimer's — moves fast around the brain's outer surfaces but roughly 50 times slower through deep tissue. Published in Science Advances, the work currently establishes baseline measurements in animal models, with aspirations to eventually compare healthy versus diseased and young versus old human brains. ScienceDaily
  • Anthropic Pitches Healthcare and Biology to IPO Investors — While Its Own Model Can't Be Used for Drug Research: Ahead of a targeted September/October IPO at a reported $965 billion valuation, Anthropic told prospective investors that expanding into healthcare and biology would help counter public skepticism about AI. Notably, the company's most capable model is explicitly barred from professional drug research use, with no disclosed timeline for when that restriction lifts — a real tension between the growth narrative and the current product. Tech Times
  • Cleveland Clinic Publishes Its Playbook for Scaling Ambient AI Scribes Across a Health System: In npj Health Systems, Cleveland Clinic detailed how it onboarded over 4,000 ambulatory clinicians to an ambient AI scribe in four months by tying vendor payment milestones directly to onboarding volume across four deployment waves. A year post-deployment, encounter-level utilization reached 70% among established users, with documentation time cut by roughly 14 minutes daily per clinician. Nature

📚 Top Research Papers

ETHOS: Towards a Modular Ethics Framework for Clinical Multi-Agent Systems

Sharma, Pugh, Beeche, Singhal, Wu, Eby et al. | arXiv 2608.15424 | August 15, 2026

ETHOS is a governance system that translates stakeholder-informed ethical requirements into executable runtime oversight for clinical AI, using a layered approach: automated checks, contextual evaluations, and an "ethics reviewer" component that monitors reasoning and can suppress problematic recommendations. Tested within a hepatology decision-support platform, ETHOS improved reliability by correctly identifying incomplete evidence and declining to recommend when safety standards couldn't be met.

Impact: This week's bacteriophage story is a stark reminder that AI capability is outpacing governance in biology broadly — ETHOS is a concrete attempt to build that governance directly into clinical AI's operational workflow rather than as an external policy layer, showing the framework can actually decline to act, not just flag concerns after the fact.

Clinical AI Governance

Characterising Cardiac Tissue Properties With Graph Neural Networks

Chiu, Kim, Saba, Mandic, Varela | arXiv 2608.15843 | August 16, 2026

A graph neural network trained on synthetic electrogram data from flat cardiac surfaces learns to identify tissue regions relevant to treating premature ventricular complexes via ablation — fibrosis (0.96 accuracy), rapid depolarization zones (0.97), and high-excitability areas (0.95). The model generalized to curved, real cardiac geometries with only minimal additional training.

Impact: Ablation procedures currently rely heavily on operator experience to identify the right tissue to target; a model this accurate at flagging arrhythmia-relevant tissue could meaningfully shorten procedure time and improve first-attempt success rates in interventional cardiology.

Cardiac AI

Towards a Physics-Informed Multiscale Digital Twin for Precision Medicine in Alzheimer's Disease

Nonn, Kerz, Wiechmann, Edison | arXiv 2608.15342 | August 15, 2026

PIM-BrainTwin borrows concepts from materials science — fatigue-related depletion, safety thresholds, critical transition points — to model an individual patient's remaining "compensatory capacity" and system stability as Alzheimer's progresses, rather than treating disease trajectory as uniform across patients. Built as an open, modular platform explicitly designed to let researchers formalize and test competing mechanistic theories against each other.

Impact: Complements this week's glymphatic-flow story nicely — both are attempts to explain why Alzheimer's progresses so differently person to person, moving the field from population-average models toward genuinely individualized disease trajectories.

Precision Medicine

CORAL: A Modality Invariant Framework for Robust Vital Sign Rate Estimation Using Correloform Analysis

Trueb, Kasbe, Srinivasan | arXiv 2608.14973 | August 15, 2026

CORAL estimates heart and respiration rate across wildly different signal types — ECG, PPG, seismocardiography, bioimpedance — using Short-Time Autocorrelation Functions to build an interpretable 2D "correloform" tracking periodicity over time. It hits near-perfect F1 scores (0.999–1.000) on fetal heart rate detection and strong correlation (r = 0.858) on neonatal respiration, without needing QRS-complex analysis.

Impact: Most vital-sign algorithms are tuned to one sensor type and break when deployed on a different one; a genuinely modality-invariant approach is exactly what's needed as vital-sign monitoring spreads from hospital-grade equipment to consumer wearables and phone cameras (see this week's GlowPulse product below).

⚠️ AI Criticism & Concerns

The AI labs asked for this exact law two months before demonstrating why it's needed

In June, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Microsoft's CEOs jointly asked Congress to mandate synthetic DNA screening. In August, a lab used exactly the kind of generative genome modeling these companies are racing to build to design 16 novel, functional viruses — and the legislation those same CEOs requested still doesn't exist. Asking for regulation while the underlying capability keeps advancing faster than any legislative process can move isn't really a solution; it's a request that shifts responsibility onto a Congress that has, so far, not acted.

A senator's letter is not a governance framework

Sanders' letters to three CEOs generated headlines, but "pause AI development" from a single senator with no accompanying legislation or enforcement mechanism is a symbolic gesture, not a policy. The actual gap this week exposed — voluntary rather than mandatory DNA synthesis screening — requires specific, technical legislation, not a general demand to slow down. The loud political response and the narrow technical fix needed are not the same thing, and conflating them risks the actual fix getting lost in the noise.

Anthropic's IPO pitch reveals the gap between narrative and product

Telling investors that healthcare and biology expansion will "improve public sentiment" about AI, while your flagship model is explicitly barred from professional drug research with no stated timeline to lift that restriction, is a genuinely awkward position. It suggests the healthcare pitch is, at least in part, a growth narrative constructed for the IPO roadshow rather than a reflection of where the product currently stands — worth remembering the next time "AI + healthcare" appears in a funding announcement instead of a product release.

Cleveland Clinic's scribe rollout worked because of governance, not because of the AI

The most important detail in Cleveland Clinic's ambient scribe success isn't the AI model — it's that they tied vendor payment milestones directly to structured onboarding waves, treating governance and phased rollout as core parts of the product rather than an afterthought. That's a genuinely replicable lesson, but it's also evidence that most AI health deployments succeed or fail on institutional discipline, not model quality — a much less exciting story than the one usually told about these rollouts.

Closing Note

Every AI-and-biology story this newsletter has covered this summer — Claude Science, BioNeMo, Biomni, ChatGPT Health — has been building toward a moment like this week's. Not because any of those tools directly caused it, but because they all represent the same underlying trajectory: AI systems moving from analyzing biological data to actively designing biological outcomes. The Evo 2 bacteriophage result is that trajectory's first unambiguous, peer-reviewed, published demonstration — and it happened in a therapeutic context, aimed at fighting antibiotic resistance, which is exactly why it's hard to simply be alarmed by it.

The uncomfortable truth is that the same capability that designed 16 useful phages this month is, mechanically, indistinguishable from the capability biosecurity researchers have been warning about for years. The screening gap between "AI designs a genome" and "someone synthesizes it" isn't a hypothetical anymore. It's the actual state of things, right now, while the legislation to close it sits unwritten.
